Read this first

What's true everywhere

There is almost no money in this product

I went looking for currency across ten screens. The dashboard table has Accounts, Ledgers, Matched, Open, Updated — not one dollar figure. The properties list has Accounts, Open, Exceptions, Last closed — none. The property record has units, square footage, year built and ownership stake — and no cash position at all.

Only the review screen carries money, and it carries it well.

This is a product about money that mostly displays counts.

The cost is concrete. "Exceptions 8" tells an accountant nothing: eight exceptions worth $12 is a Friday afternoon, eight worth $80,000 is an emergency, and the screen renders them identically. On the properties list, 908 Larkin shows 168 in amber and you cannot tell whether that's a rounding problem or a disaster.

The fix runs through every screen below: show dollars at risk, and sort by them. Counts are a secondary detail; the unexplained difference is the headline. This one change does more than any other single thing to make the product read as something a controller would use.

Tokens follow the accountant everywhere

The AGENTS panel — Success rate and Tokens — is pinned bottom-left on every screen in the product. So does "Tokens used 1.24M" on the dashboard hero. An accountant doesn't know what a token is and can't act on one.

It's also visibly broken: the panel is clipped in all ten screenshots — "S…ary 89%", "…etails ›". That's the first thing a viewer notices on camera.

Remove it from every screen except AI Performance, and express cost there in dollars per reconciliation.

The hero numbers describe the machine, not the work

"First-pass accuracy 90%" and "Tokens used 1.24M" are the two biggest things on the dashboard. Neither answers the question the person opened the app to ask, which is always one of three: is my month done, what needs me, and what's blocking me.

One account is being treated like all the others, and it shouldn't be

On the property record, three accounts sit in an identical list: Chase Operating, Wells Fargo Reserve, and BoA Security Deposit.

Security deposits are frequently legally segregated funds. Showing them in the same list, with the same treatment and the same automation, is the kind of thing that ends a conversation with a real property manager. Mark those accounts, scope automation away from them, and say so out loud — it converts a risk into evidence of judgement.

The biggest single problem

"Reconciled 0 · Open 0 · Net difference $0.00" is tucked into the top of a side panel at small type. That is the most important number in the entire product — the thing that decides whether the month can close — and it is a caption.

Meanwhile the centre of the screen, the largest and most prominent area, holds a decorative orb between four account cards that say "Clear" with a tick. Clear of what? No balance, no difference, no evidence.

Swap them. The canvas should carry the proof; the agents belong in the rail.

Prompt · reconciliation canvas
Rebuild the reconciliation canvas so the balance proof is the main event and
the agent visualisation moves to a supporting role.

The unit is ONE ACCOUNT FOR ONE PERIOD, not the session. So the canvas shows a

  - statement closing balance
  - book balance

A card saying "Clear" with a tick and no number is not evidence — every state

Below the strip, show the session-level roll-up: "3 of 4 accounts proven ·

Screen three

Review records

The strongest screen in the product, and the closest to right. It has money, it has per-record reasons — "Matched on memo reference · exact amount", "Counterparty and amount match · 1 day apart" — and it has the net difference at the top. That reasoning line is genuinely good design and you should point at it in the walkthrough.

Keep
  • The per-record reason line. This is the product's best idea.
  • Net difference in the stats band.
  • "This session is posted. Its records are a record of what was decided." — precise, well-written, does real work.
  • The bank-logo filter chips.
Kill
  • The bare confidence percentage. 92%, 99%, 84%, 77% — of what? And it sits on approved records, where it changes nothing.
  • "Approved" as a label. Your own vocabulary contract says Matched.
  • Three filters where the domain needs five.
Add
  • The ledger side of every row. A match has two sides; the row shows one.
  • Resolution actions beyond approve and flag.
  • Sort by what deserves attention, not by date.
  • A link from "Net difference" to the full proof.
Prompt · review records
Rework the review list against the revised match contract.

Every row shows BOTH SIDES of the match, not just the bank line. Collapsed:
bank line on the left, matched ledger row on the right, the rule that joined
them in the middle. Expanded: all candidates considered, including the ones
rejected and why.

For an ambiguous record, the expanded row lets the reviewer PICK a candidate —
radio-style, then confirm. This is currently impossible and it is the most
common action in the job. Make it obvious that the choice matters: state that
the candidate not picked becomes an open item carried into next month.

Replace the three filters with the five outcomes: Matched · Only on bank ·
Only in ledger · Will clear soon · Needs fixing. Rename "Approved" to
"Matched" to match the vocabulary contract.

Replace the bare confidence chip. On a matched row show WHY it matched
(the rule that fired) and how many candidates there were — that is a fact, not
a feeling. Delete the percentage from matched rows entirely. Where a number
is genuinely useful, label the question it answers in words.

Add the four resolution actions to exception rows: accept as timing, correct
the match, add a correcting entry, set aside with a reason. Each must visibly
change the net difference at the top of the screen. Approving must no longer
be a valid response to a real difference.

Default sort becomes: needs a decision first, then by absolute amount
descending. Date is a secondary sort. Make "Net difference" open the full
balance proof.

Screen five

Property record

Quietly the most credible screen you have. The associated accounts section carries the GL account and the routing number — real integration detail that most designers would never think to show. Keep all of it.

Keep
  • Associated accounts with GL mapping and routing numbers. Genuinely convincing.
  • Ledger identity block — legal entity, property code, ledger property ID, ledger source.
  • The sessions history table with who ran it.
Kill
  • The security deposit account sitting in an identical row to the operating account.
Add
  • Cash position per account. A property record with no balances is a brochure.
  • Open items carried, per account, with ages.
  • The per-bank transaction code dictionary.
  • Dollars in the sessions history.

The code dictionary is the piece nobody would think of. Bank statement type codes are not fully standard — banks vary, and publish proprietary codes especially in the 900s. So "this code means a returned deposit" is a per-bank mapping that has to live somewhere, be versioned, and be recorded against each run. This screen is where it belongs, and putting it here is the kind of detail that makes an engineer believe you've read a real file.

Prompt · property record
Add the financial reality to the property record.

On each associated account, show: current book balance, last statement closing
balance, open items carried (count and total), and the date last proven. An
account row without balances tells the accountant nothing.

Give security-deposit accounts a visually distinct treatment with a short
explanatory line — these are frequently legally segregated funds, they are
excluded from automation, and the record should say so rather than listing
them identically to operating cash.

Add a per-account "statement code mapping" section: the bank's transaction
type codes and what each one means to us, with a version and a last-edited
date. Codes vary by bank and include proprietary ranges, so this is
configuration, not a constant.

Add dollars to the sessions history table: unexplained at first pass, and
unexplained at close. "Matched 295 / 303" with no money doesn't say whether
that month went well.

Screen six

AI performance

Keep
  • The token split by agent. This is the right home for it.
  • Records per reviewer hour as a trend — a real productivity measure.
  • Average duration per agent.
Kill
  • "Success rate" per agent. Success at what? An agent finishing isn't success.
  • Two unexplained trends pointing the wrong way on a page arguing improvement.
Add
  • Override rate. The most informative number in the system and it's absent.
  • Escaped errors.
  • Cost in dollars per reconciliation.
  • Rule and autonomy health.

Two numbers are quietly undermining this page. Records matched is down 19% since December, and tokens per record are up 49% — sitting on a screen whose purpose is to argue the system is improving, with no explanation offered.

Don't fix the data. Design the surface that explains a bad trend, because a page that only looks good when the numbers are good is one nobody trusts when they aren't. Being able to say "matched volume fell because two properties were sold" is a stronger product than one that never shows a decline.

Prompt · AI performance
Rebuild this page around trust rather than throughput.

Lead with OVERRIDE RATE — how often a person disagreed with the machine, as a
trend. Rising means drift or stale rules. It is the most informative number in
the system and it is currently missing entirely.

Add ESCAPED ERRORS: mistakes found after posting, or found by sampling
already-approved work. Show it even when it is zero, because zero with a
sampling rate beside it is a claim, and zero with nothing beside it is an
absence of looking.

Replace "success rate" per agent with something that means something: for
Reading, the share of statements whose extracted rows reconciled to the
statement's own printed totals. For Matching, first-pass match rate frozen at
the machine's verdict. For Explaining, how often its top-ranked candidate was
the one the human picked.

Express cost in dollars per reconciliation as the primary figure, with tokens
as the secondary engineering detail beneath it.

Every trend that moves the wrong way needs an annotation slot. Records matched
is down 19% and tokens per record up 49% with no explanation — build the
affordance that lets a cause be attached to a movement, and populate it.

Add a rule health panel: rules by how often they fire, how often they're
overridden, which have not fired in three months, and which contradict each
other.

Screen seven

Knowledge base

The most sophisticated thinking in the product. Rules written in prose, scoped to a property or to all properties, with hit counts per cycle and a "not applied this cycle" state. That's a well-considered learning loop and it's the thing that most distinguishes this from a dashboard.

It is also, right now, the most dangerous screen — and that's a compliment, because only a design that does something real can be dangerous.

Read your own rule list again. One of them says:

"Tenant ACH returns for unit 308 are a known recurring issue with broken auto-pay. Approve…"

That is a bounced rent payment — and the rule instructs the system to approve it. Approving a returned payment tells the books the tenant paid when they didn't. The correct handling is to recognise the pattern and book the reversal and the fee.

Your knowledge base contains a rule that automates the exact domain error the whole teardown was about. Nobody would have caught it, because nothing checks a rule against the domain — and that is precisely the point to make in the case study.

Two others deserve the same scrutiny. "Refunds under $250 are routine. Auto-approve and skip the ambiguity check" changes how money is handled, in free text, with no author and no approval. And "Wire transfers to title companies are always legitimate. Do not flag as unknown counterparty" — "always" is a dangerous word in a system that moves money, and a rule that switches off a check is exactly the rule that needs the strongest gate.

Keep
  • Prose rules. Readable by the person whose knowledge they encode.
  • Property scope versus portfolio scope.
  • "Applied 6× this cycle" and "Not applied this cycle".
  • Active / Archived.
  • The session-level panel, which is better than the global one.
Kill
  • Creating a rule with a single click and no review.
  • "In use" as the only usage state on the global list.
  • Rules with no author and no expiry.
Add
  • A preview: what this rule would have changed last month.
  • Author, approver, expiry.
  • Override tracking and automatic demotion.
  • Conflict detection at the moment of approval.
  • A distinction between rules that add caution and rules that remove it.
Prompt · knowledge base
Turn rules from settings into governed changes.

Give every rule a lifecycle: proposed → previewed → approved → active →
measured → retired. The PREVIEW is the piece that's missing and it matters
most: before a rule goes live, show what it would have done to last month —
"this would have changed 14 records, $3,120". Nobody should activate a rule
that changes how money is handled without seeing that.

Every rule carries: who wrote it, who approved it, what it applies to, when it
expires, how often it has fired, and how often a human has overridden it.
A rule overridden repeatedly surfaces itself as wrong. A rule that hasn't
fired in three months surfaces itself as dead.

Classify rules by direction and treat them differently. A rule that ADDS
caution ("send it for review instead") is low risk. A rule that REMOVES a
check — "auto-approve and skip the ambiguity check", "always legitimate, do
not flag" — is high risk, and needs a stronger gate, a shorter expiry, and a
visual treatment that says so.

Detect conflicts at approval time. Two rules that disagree about the same
situation must be surfaced before the second one goes live.

Add per-rule autonomy level: observed, proposed, provisional, autonomous, with
a sampling rate that RISES as autonomy rises. Demotion is automatic when the
override rate crosses a threshold.

Finally, fix the seeded rule about tenant ACH returns. It currently instructs
the system to approve a bounced rent payment, which would tell the books a
tenant paid when they didn't. Replace it with a rule that recognises the
return-plus-fee pattern and books the reversal. Then add a check that warns
when a proposed rule would auto-approve a returned item.

Finally

Sequence

The screens depend on each other, so order matters more than usual. Do the playbook's data-contract prompts (1 and 2) before any of these — otherwise you'll build interfaces against a model that can't hold what they need to show.

  • Review records first. It's closest to right, and getting the two-sided match and the five outcomes working here proves the new contract before it spreads.
  • Then the run canvas, because the balance proof is the piece everything else links to.
  • Then dashboard and properties together — they're the same change twice, and doing them as a pair keeps the columns consistent.
  • Then the property record, which is additive and low-risk.
  • Knowledge base and AI performance last — they need the override data that only exists once the review screen is producing it.

One honest note on all of this. These screens are well made — the composition, the restraint, the reasoning lines on the review rows. The problem was never craft. It was that a product about money had almost stopped showing money, and once you put the dollars back, most of what's above follows on its own.
