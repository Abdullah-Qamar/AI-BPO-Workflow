Design teardown · property accounting

Where the design breaks against the real work

Eight gaps between how Reconciler models bank reconciliation and how bank reconciliation actually behaves. Each one is a design decision with a consequence for whoever builds it.

The root of all eight: the design describes comparing two lists of transactions. Real reconciliation is proving that two balances agree, and that every difference between them is explained. Those are different jobs, and the second one needs things the first never asks for.

Ordered by severity, not by workflow. Gap 1 is structural — fixing it makes 2, 3 and 5 much easier. Gaps 7 and 8 are small and quick.

Gap 01Structural

The reviewer can't fix a match

Today
When the system is unsure, it says "I found two possible matches and can't choose." The only buttons are approve and flag. The two candidates exist only as words inside a note — so there is nothing to click.
Why it hurts
Choosing the right answer when the machine is unsure is the most common thing a human does in this job. The product asks the question and then gives the person nowhere to answer it.
Fix
Make a match a real object with two sides — a bank side and a ledger side that can hold more than one row. Then the candidates become selectable, and one click resolves it.

Gap 02High

Two buckets can't hold a normal month

Today
Every item lands in one of two piles: approved, or flagged as a problem.
Why it hurts
A cheque written on the 28th usually hasn't cleared the bank yet. It matches nothing — and that is completely normal. With two piles it has to be called a problem. Once half the flags turn out to be nothing, people stop trusting the flag, and the product stops working.
Fix
Five outcomes instead of two, so "normal but unmatched" has its own home and the flag keeps its meaning.

Gap 03High

Problems have nowhere to go

Today
A reviewer can move an item between the two piles and write a comment. That's the whole toolkit.
Why it hurts
The bank says $1,200 and the books say $1,275. Moving that to approved does not make the $75 go away — the books are still wrong by $75, and the system then sends it onward as approved. The product creates the feeling of finishing without the substance of it.
Fix
Four real ways out, each one visibly changing the numbers. Approve should never be the answer to a genuine difference.

Gap 04High · biggest addition

The proof the month is closed is missing

Today
The product shows counts, percentages and a "tied" label. It never shows the bank's balance beside the books' balance, the gap between them, and what explains the gap.
Why it hurts
That single view is the whole reason reconciliation exists, and it is what a controller signs. Without it the product looks like a clever sorting tool rather than something that closes a month.
Fix
One summary panel, ending in the number that matters: how much is still unexplained. When it reaches zero, the month is done.

Gap 05Medium · best story

A bounced payment is treated as a mystery

Today
One of your own sample items is a tenant's payment being returned. The design reports it as "amounts don't match — $1,200 versus $1,275" and leaves the person to work it out.
Why it hurts
That $75 is almost certainly the bank's fee for the bounced payment. This is an ordinary event that happens to property managers constantly — and the design has no idea it exists, so it labels a routine thing as a red flag. An accountant would spot that in seconds. The original payment is also sitting elsewhere with no link, so the person only ever sees half the story.
Fix
Recognise the pattern by name and show the three parts as one linked event. A scary flag becomes a two-second read.

Gap 06Medium · deepest build impact

Every month starts from nothing

Today
Each month is treated as: take two files, compare them, done. Next month, two new files, start fresh.
Why it hurts
A cheque written on 28 March might not clear until May. It has to stay visible month after month until it does — and if it is still sitting there after nine months, that is its own problem worth raising. Right now it simply disappears.
Fix
Carry unfinished items forward with their age. This is the gap with the largest effect on whoever builds it: it turns the product from "compare two files" into "keep a running record per bank account", which changes the foundation.

Gap 07Quick · contradicts your own argument

The one step that can't fail is the dangerous one

Today
Reading the files can fail, and there is a proper screen for it. Sending everything to the accounting system has no failure state at all. It only succeeds.
Why it hurts
This is backwards. Reading a file is safe and easy to repeat. Writing into the accounting system is the step that permanently changes something — most likely to go wrong, hardest to undo. If it stops halfway, some records are in and some aren't, and nobody knows which. It also contradicts your own case study, which argues that this step deserves the strongest protection because it can't be taken back.
Fix
A half-finished state, a retry that can't send anything twice, and a visible undo. The undo is the strongest trust feature in the whole product.

Gap 08Quick

One percentage, two meanings

Today
The same confidence percentage appears on approved items and on flagged items.
Why it hurts
Those answer different questions — "how sure am I this match is right" and "how unusual does this transaction look". A low number might mean the system is guessing, or it might mean something looks suspicious. Those need opposite responses, and the person can't tell which they're looking at.
Fix
Pick one meaning, put it in words next to the number, and never show the other one in the same place.

What to do with this

The order I'd work in

  1. Give a match two sides. Gaps 2, 3 and 5 all get easier once this is done.
  2. Design the three screens it unlocks: the balance proof, the pick-the-right-match row, and the correction flow.
  3. Close the quick contradictions — the two different send buttons, the four names for two numbers, the unlabelled percentage.
  4. Then write the case study, built on findings you can defend out loud.

A designer who found eight holes in their own work is more convincing than one who claims there were none. The holes are the evidence that you know how to look — but only once you can explain each one to a sceptical accountant without notes.
