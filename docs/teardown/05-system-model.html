Third of three · system model

Objects, gates and evidence

What things exist and how they nest, who is allowed to do what, how corrections travel back, and what the system has to be able to prove afterwards.

The single biggest structural correction: your design has one tree, and it needs two. Properties and accounts are a standing world that changes rarely. Periods and reconciliations are a moving world that happens every month. They are different shapes with different lifespans.

And the interesting design problem is not either tree — it is the two places where they touch.

Taxonomy

Two worlds, and where they meet

The standing world is set up once and edited on rare events — a refinance, a new escrow account, a property sold. The moving world is created fresh every cycle and then frozen forever as a record of what happened.

Mixing them is what makes products like this confusing to use and painful to build. A bank account is not a thing that "belongs to a session"; a session borrows it.

The hierarchy bug

A session is a package, not a unit

In your design a session covers one property and contains four bank accounts. But a session never ties out — each account ties out separately. You cannot prove a property's cash agrees; you can only prove each account agrees, four times.

So the session is the wrong unit to hang the work on. The unit is one account for one month. The property-level thing above it is a package that completes when all of its accounts have completed — useful for tracking, useless for proving.

Human in the loop

Four roles, and three different kinds of involvement

Your prototype has no roles at all, which means the person who prepares the work can also approve and post it. In accounting that is not a missing feature — it is a missing control. Separating preparation from approval is one of the oldest rules in the trade, because it is what stops one person from both making and hiding a mistake.

Preparer · the property accountant

Uploads the documents, works the exception queue, resolves items, writes adjustments, hands the package up.

Cannot approve their own package. Cannot post. Cannot approve a rule.

Approver · the controller

Reviews the balance proof and the resolutions, signs the package, authorises posting. Approves new rules.

Cannot approve a package they prepared themselves.

Administrator · setup and mappings

Maintains properties, bank accounts and which ledger line each one posts to. Changing a ledger mapping quietly changes where money lands, so it is its own privileged act with its own record.

Observer · auditor, owner, read-only

Can see everything and change nothing — including how a past month was decided, as it stood at the time.

Not all human involvement is review

This is the distinction your design is missing, and it's the one that makes an AI product feel considered rather than nervous.

  • DecideThe person is the only one who can settle it. Picking the right match from two candidates. The system genuinely does not know.
  • AuthoriseThe system already decided; the person takes responsibility for it. Posting. The work here is not thinking, it is accepting consequences — so the screen's job is to make the consequences plain.
  • SampleThe person deliberately checks work nobody flagged. Not because it's suspect, but because a reviewer who only ever sees exceptions slowly forgets what normal looks like — and because it's the only way to find the mistakes the system never noticed.

That third one is worth designing properly. The better this system gets, the less practice its reviewers get, and the worse they become at catching the failures that do slip through. A small, scheduled sample of already-approved work is how you counter that. It is also the only place your escaped-error number can come from.

What happens when nobody acts

Worth designing before anyone asks: a reconciliation sitting unreviewed for five days while the period is about to close is a real operational state, and right now the product has nothing to say about it. Ageing, nudging and escalating are part of the workflow, not an afterthought.

Feedback

One correction, four destinations

You currently have one channel: a correction becomes a written rule. That's a good instinct, but four genuinely different things are being collapsed into it — with different owners, different speeds and different risks.

Rules are data. Prompts are code. The moment a user's correction edits a prompt, nobody can say why the system behaved differently last Tuesday.

A rule has a life, not just a birth

Your design creates rules and counts how often they fire. That's the start. What's missing is everything after: a rule that gets overridden often is wrong, a rule that stops firing is dead, and a rule nobody owns should not outlive the person who wrote it.

The loop runs both ways

Almost every design of this kind forgets the return journey. If someone's correction changed the system, tell them — and tell them what it did. "The rule you approved in April has matched 31 items since, and been overridden twice." That one sentence is what turns a correction from a chore into a contribution, and it is the cheapest trust-building feature in the product.

Observability

One record, three audiences, three units

Your dashboard currently shows an accountant that 1.24 million tokens were used. They do not know what a token is, cannot act on it, and are mildly alarmed. That's not a placement mistake — it's a sign the system has one view where it needs three.

The run produces a single record of what happened. Each audience reads that record in the unit they think in.

Observing isn't enough — you have to be able to reconstruct

This is the part that separates an accounting product from a dashboard. Eighteen months from now someone will ask why a particular match happened in March. Answering that means the March run has to be replayable as it stood then — even though the rules have changed six times since.

So every run permanently records: which documents came in and a fingerprint proving they weren't swapped, which rules were active and at which version, which model and prompt version ran, every suggestion the model made and whether it was accepted, every human action with a name and a time, and every posting attempt with its outcome.

That list is not paranoia. It's the difference between "the system says so" and "here is what happened."

One rule that governs all of them: every quality number must belong to either the machine or the human, never both. A metric that improves when people work harder is not measuring the system. You already got this right once, deliberately — make it a stated principle, because it's the kind of thing an interviewer will stop and ask about.

Explain the awkward numbers rather than hiding them

Your current figures show matches falling and cost per record rising, with no explanation. The instinct is to fix the data. The better instinct is to design the surface that explains a bad trend — because a system that only looks good when the numbers are good is a system nobody will trust when they aren't.

In one line each

What to take away

Taxonomy. Two worlds, not one. The unit of work is an account for a month, not a session for a property.

Humans. Four roles, a hard line between preparing and approving, and three distinct kinds of involvement — deciding, authorising, and sampling.

Feedback. One correction, four destinations. Rules are data, prompts are code. And tell people what their correction did.

Observability. One record, three audiences, three units. Reconstruct, don't just observe. And watch the override rate above everything else.

None of this requires you to build a backend. All of it is design work — objects, permissions, states and screens — and it is exactly the design work that separates someone who has thought about AI products from someone who has drawn one.
