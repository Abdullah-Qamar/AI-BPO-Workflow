Companion to the gap teardown

Where the agents belong

Once the eight gaps are fixed, the AI has less to do — and what's left is far easier to defend. This is the map of which steps a model should touch, and which it must never touch.

The dividing line is reproducibility. If a controller has to be able to re-run a step and get the identical answer, and defend that answer to an auditor, a model cannot do it. If the input is messy human language and the output is a sentence for a person to read, a model is the only sensible tool.

Almost everything follows from that one line.

The model never decides what is true. It decides what to read, and what to say.

The roster

Four jobs, two of them not intelligent at all

You currently have three agents named after stages. These four are named after the kind of thinking each one does — which is the only naming that survives a hard question.

1 · The Reader

Model, checked

Turns a bank statement and a ledger export into clean rows of date, amount, description and reference — whatever shape they arrived in.

Why a model earns this
There are roughly 8,900 banks in the United States, and a small property owner is more likely to bank with a local one than a national one. Every statement is laid out differently. No set of templates survives contact with that, and this is exactly what models are good at.
The guard that makes it safe
Every statement states its own opening balance, closing balance and totals. The rows the Reader produces must reproduce those figures exactly. If they don't, the read failed and the run stops — the product refuses to reconcile something it isn't sure it read correctly.
Never
Never invents, corrects or tidies a figure it couldn't read. An unreadable line is reported as unreadable.

2 · The Matcher

No model at all

Pairs bank rows with ledger rows by working down a written list of rules: same amount same day, then same amount within a few days, then amount plus reference number, then one deposit against several ledger rows that add up to it.

Why no model
This is the step where being wrong costs real money, and where "why did these two match?" must have an answer an auditor accepts. A rule gives that answer. A model gives an opinion that may differ next Tuesday.
What this fixes
Certainty stops being a feeling and becomes a fact: which rule fired, and how many candidates it found. That is Gap 8 solved at the root.
Bonus
Because it's rules, you can test it. Run it against a month someone already closed by hand and count what it got right. That is an honest accuracy number, and right now you don't have one.

3 · The Explainer

Model, no authority

Takes everything that didn't match cleanly and does three things: recognises the common patterns by name, ranks the possible answers, and writes the one-line reason the person reads.

Why a model earns this
"Both rows match the amount — but row 142 shares the payment reference and row 154 is three days closer" is writing. It is the one job here that is genuinely language work, and it is what turns a wall of exceptions into a queue somebody can get through.
The guard
It proposes; it never commits. A pattern is only shown as fact once a rule has confirmed it — "bounced payment" appears only if the original payment is actually found and the amounts add up. A guess the machine can't back up is shown as a guess.
Never
Never moves an item into approved. Never touches the balance proof. Never decides anything a person would have to defend.

4 · The Poster

No model at all

Sends approved entries into the accounting system, one at a time, each carrying a tag so that sending it twice is impossible. Handles the half-finished case. Supports undo.

Why no model
Nothing here is a judgement. It is the most consequential step in the product and the least intelligent, and those two facts are related.

The idea worth stealing

The Reader marks its own work

This is the design move that makes model-based document reading acceptable in an accounting product, and it costs almost nothing: the document already tells you the right answer.

A bank statement prints its opening balance, its closing balance, and its totals. So after the Reader has extracted the rows, you add them up and compare. If they agree, the read was complete. If they don't, something was missed — and the honest response is to stop, not to reconcile against a half-read statement.

How they share one decision

Three participants, one ambiguous item

Follow the ambiguous refund from Gap 1 all the way through. Notice that each participant does only what it is good at, and that the person's click is the only thing that settles it.

The dangerous one

Learning from corrections needs a gate

The learning loop is the most AI-native part of your product and the part most likely to get it rejected by a real finance team. A free-text rule like "auto-approve refunds under $250" changes how money is handled — and at the moment anyone can write one, unsigned, unreviewed, with no way to see what it would do.

The fix is not to remove it. It is to treat a rule as something that gets proposed, previewed and approved — like any other change to how money is handled.

Back to the screen

What the person should see

Keep the lanes, change the labels

People do need to see the work moving — that part of your design is right. But name the lanes after the work, not after the machine: Reading, Matching, Reviewing, Posting. Nobody outside your team knows what "Agent 2" is.

Show which parts had an opinion

Mark each result with how it was decided. On the balance proof, the words "calculated, not estimated" are a genuine trust feature — accountants relax visibly when told a number came from arithmetic. Right now your product hides its most trustworthy quality behind a shimmer.

Stop calling it three agents

What you have is a workflow with intelligent steps, not a team of agents making plans. Saying that plainly is more accurate and more credible — the vaguer claim is what invites the sceptical question in the first place. It also matches the current industry distinction between workflows and agents, which the technically literate person interviewing you will know.

The shape of the answer

Less AI, better product

You started with three agents doing everything. You end with a model at each edge — one reading documents nobody can template, one writing the sentences that make a queue manageable — and rules in the middle, where the money is.

The strongest thing you can say in an interview about an AI product is which part you took the AI out of, and why. Almost nobody says it. It signals that you understand what these systems are bad at, which is the rarer and more valuable knowledge.
